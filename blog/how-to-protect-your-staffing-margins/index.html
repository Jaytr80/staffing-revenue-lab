How to Protect Your Staffing Margins When Clients Push Back on Bill Rates

By Jay Tyler · November 3, 2026

Every client will ask you to lower your rate at some point. The firms that keep healthy margins aren't the ones who never get asked. They're the ones who are ready for the conversation.

Rate pressure is part of staffing. A new procurement lead wants savings. A competitor quoted lower. Budgets got tight. The easy move is to drop your markup and keep the account. But a few points of margin, given away on every order, can be the difference between a desk that grows and one that just stays busy.

Here's how to handle bill rate pushback without losing the client or your profit.

Step 1: Know your real numbers before the call

You can't defend a rate you don't fully understand. Before any pricing conversation, know:

  • Your true burden: payroll taxes, workers' comp, insurance, benefits and any required paid leave
  • Your gross margin per hour on each order, not just your markup percentage
  • The lowest rate you'll accept for this role, and why

If you don't know your floor before the call, you'll find it during the call, and it will usually be lower than it should be.

Step 2: Ask what's behind the request

"Can you do better on the rate?" isn't always about the rate. Ask a simple question before you answer: "Help me understand what's driving that. Is it budget, a competing quote or something else?"

The answer changes your response:

  • A budget cap might mean adjusting the role, the shift or the start date instead of your margin
  • A competing quote is a chance to compare what each firm actually delivers
  • A new buyer looking for quick savings often cares more about predictability than the lowest number

Step 3: Sell the cost of the open role, not the hourly rate

Clients compare rates because rates are easy to compare. Your job is to move the conversation to what they actually care about: getting the work done.

Talk about what an unfilled or badly filled role costs them:

  • Overtime for the team covering the gap
  • Missed production, late shipments or slower service
  • Turnover when the wrong person starts and leaves in two weeks

A lower rate from a firm that can't fill the order or keep people on the job is not a savings. Say that plainly, with examples from your own work.

Step 4: Trade, don't discount

If you decide to move on price, get something in return. A discount with nothing attached teaches the client that pushing works. Some fair trades:

  • A lower rate in exchange for higher volume or exclusivity on a role
  • Faster feedback and interview turnaround so your recruiters waste less time
  • Longer assignment commitments or a defined conversion fee
  • Net payment terms that help your cash flow

A simple line works: "I can look at the rate if we can lock in exclusivity on these roles for the next 90 days."

Step 5: Know when to walk away

Not every order is worth keeping. Some orders cost you more in recruiter time, turnover and risk than they bring in. If a client insists on a rate below your floor, it's okay to say so respectfully:

"I want to help, but at that rate I can't recruit the quality of people you need or keep them on the job. If things change, I'd be glad to revisit it."

Losing a low-margin order frees your team for clients who value the work. And sometimes the client comes back once the cheaper option doesn't deliver.

Step 6: Raise rates on purpose, not by accident

Margin erodes quietly when rates never move but costs do. Build a habit:

  • Review every active account's rates at least once a year
  • Raise rates when wages, burden or required benefits go up, and explain the reason in writing
  • Quote new orders at current market pay, not last year's

Clients accept increases more easily when they're expected, explained and tied to keeping good people on the job.

Use AI to prepare, not to negotiate for you

AI can help you get ready. Ask it to summarize a client's hiring history, list likely objections for a pricing call or draft a short email explaining a rate increase. Then add your real numbers and your own voice. The relationship is still yours to manage.

The bottom line

Protecting margin isn't about being rigid. It's about knowing your numbers, understanding why the client is asking, showing the real cost of an open role and trading instead of giving discounts away. Do that consistently, and you'll keep both the client and the profit.
