How to Use AI Prompts for Recruiting Business Development (Without Sounding Like Everyone Else)

AI can save you hours on BD. It can also make you sound exactly like every other recruiter in your client's inbox. The difference is what you give it before you hit enter.

Most recruiters I talk to have tried AI for business development. They asked it to "write a prospecting email to a VP of Engineering," got something polished and generic, sent a few, and heard nothing back. Then they decided AI doesn't work for BD.

The tool wasn't the problem. The input was. A prompt is a work order. If the work order is vague, the output is vague. If the work order carries your market knowledge, your angle, and a clear job to do, AI becomes a fast, tireless assistant for the parts of BD that eat your week.

Here is how I use prompts across the BD cycle, and the habits that keep the output sounding like you.

Start with one line of judgment

Before you open a prompt, write one line for each target account: why them, why now. Maybe they posted three similar roles in a month. Maybe they opened a new location. Maybe a hiring manager you placed with moved there.

That line is the part AI can't supply. It doesn't know your market, your history, or which signals actually matter in your niche. When you paste that line into a prompt, everything downstream gets sharper. When you skip it, you get filler.

A simple rule: if you can't write the "why now" line, you're not ready to reach out to that account yet. Do the research first.

Use prompts in four places

The 25 free prompts I put together are grouped around the parts of the desk where AI actually saves time. You can use the same structure with any prompts you write yourself.

1. Finding and prioritizing accounts

AI is good at organizing what you already know. Give it your list of target companies with notes on open roles, size, and past contact, and ask it to rank them by fit and urgency with a short reason for each. You still make the call, but you start the day with a ranked list instead of a blank page.

You can also ask it to suggest what to look for: which signals in job postings, press releases, or org changes tend to mean a company will need outside help. Then you go verify.

2. Client outreach

This is where most people start, and where generic output hurts the most. A better outreach prompt includes:

  • Who you're writing to and their likely priorities
  • Your "why them, why now" line
  • What you can credibly offer (a specialty, a talent pool, a relevant placement you can talk about)
  • The length and tone you want (short, plain, no hype)
  • One clear, low-pressure ask

Then ask for three versions and pick the one closest to how you talk. Edit it. Read it out loud. If you wouldn't say it on the phone, don't send it.

3. Discovery and job order prep

Before a call, give AI the job description and what you know about the company, and ask it for the questions a strong recruiter would ask to understand the real need: why the role is open, what happens if it stays open, who decides, and what has already been tried.

You'll still lead the conversation. But walking in with a sharp question list is the difference between taking an order and shaping one.

4. Candidate outreach and follow-up

AI is useful for turning a dry job description into a message a candidate might actually read, and for drafting follow-ups that add something new instead of "just checking in." Give it the role's real selling points, which you learned in discovery, not the boilerplate from the posting.

Habits that keep AI output useful

Give context, not just commands. "Write a follow-up" gets you filler. "Write a two-sentence follow-up to a hiring manager who said budget is frozen until next quarter; reference the backfill risk she mentioned" gets you something usable.

Ask for options, then choose. Three short drafts beat one long one. You're the editor, not the typist.

Set guardrails. Tell it what to avoid: buzzwords, exclamation points, claims you can't back up, and anything longer than you'd read yourself.

Keep your own facts in charge. Never let AI invent a placement, a statistic, or a client name. If it adds something you can't verify, cut it.

Protect private information. Don't paste candidate personal details or confidential client information into tools your firm hasn't approved. Summarize instead.

Save what works. When a prompt produces something you'd actually send, save it with a note on when to use it. Over time you build your own prompt library that fits your desk.

A simple weekly routine

You don't need a big system to start. Try this for one week:

  1. Monday: Write your "why them, why now" line for ten target accounts. Use a prompt to rank them.
  2. Tuesday: Draft outreach for the top five using the outreach structure above. Edit and send.
  3. Wednesday: Prep discovery questions for any call on your calendar.
  4. Thursday: Draft follow-ups that add one new piece of value each.
  5. Friday: Review what got replies. Save the prompts that worked and adjust the ones that didn't.

That's it. One week of this will tell you more about how AI fits your desk than a month of reading about it.

The bottom line

AI won't build client relationships for you. It will give you back the hours you spend staring at a blank email, so you can spend them on calls and conversations that actually produce job orders. Bring the judgment. Let AI handle the first draft.
