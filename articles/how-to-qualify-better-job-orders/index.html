How to Qualify Better Job Orders (and Stop Working Reqs You Can't Fill)

Not every job order deserves your time. The recruiters who make the most placements are often the ones who say "not yet" to the most reqs.

Every recruiter knows the feeling. You take a new job order, start sourcing, submit a few strong candidates, and then it goes quiet. The hiring manager is "reviewing." The role gets put on hold. Or it gets filled internally while you're still waiting for feedback.

That's not bad luck. It's usually a qualification problem. When every job order gets the same effort, the weak ones quietly take time away from the ones you could actually fill. Better qualification is one of the simplest ways to raise your fill rate without working more hours.

Why job order quality matters more than volume

A desk full of open reqs feels productive. But your time is limited, and it gets split across everything on the board. A smaller number of well-qualified orders, where the need is real, the decision-maker is engaged and the terms work, usually produces more placements than a long list of maybes.

Qualifying isn't about turning clients away. It's about knowing which orders get your best effort now, which need more information, and which you should politely pass on.

The four things to qualify

I look at every job order through four questions. If you can't answer them, you don't really understand the order yet.

1. Urgency: Is there a real reason this has to be filled?

Ask what happens if the role stays open for another 60 days. Is a project delayed? Is the team covering extra work? Is revenue at risk? A role with a clear cost of staying open gets attention from the client. A "nice to have" usually doesn't.

Questions to ask:

  • Why is this role open now?
  • What's the impact on the team or business if it stays open?
  • When do you need someone started, and what's driving that date?

2. Access: Can you reach the person who decides?

If you can only talk to someone who passes information along, feedback will be slow and context will be thin. Strong orders come with access to the hiring manager, or at least a commitment to it.

Questions to ask:

  • Who makes the final decision?
  • Can I have a short call with the hiring manager to understand the role?
  • How fast can you give feedback on submittals?

3. Realism: Can this role be filled as written?

Many hard-to-fill orders are hard because the requirements, pay and location don't match the market. Your job is to say that early and clearly, with what you know about the talent pool.

Questions to ask:

  • Which requirements are must-haves and which are preferences?
  • Is the pay range set, or is there flexibility for the right person?
  • Is remote or hybrid work possible?
  • Have you interviewed anyone yet? What was missing?

If the answers point to a mismatch, share what you're seeing in the market and suggest adjustments. That conversation alone often builds more trust than a fast submittal.

4. Terms: Does the deal work for both sides?

Fee, exclusivity, competition and process all affect whether an order is worth your time. A role shared with many agencies on contingency, with slow feedback, is a very different bet than one you're working with clear commitment.

Questions to ask:

  • How many other firms are working on this?
  • Is there an internal candidate or referral in process?
  • What does the interview process look like, start to finish?
  • Are you open to an exclusive window or a retained arrangement?

Score every order before you work it

Turn the four questions into a quick score. Rate each from 1 to 5:

  • Urgency: 1 = no real deadline, 5 = clear business cost to staying open
  • Access: 1 = no hiring manager contact, 5 = direct line and quick feedback
  • Realism: 1 = requirements and pay don't match the market, 5 = fillable as written
  • Terms: 1 = crowded and uncommitted, 5 = exclusive or committed with a clear process

Add them up. Then sort your board:

  • 16 to 20: Work it now. These get your best sourcing time.
  • 11 to 15: Work it, but go back and fix the weakest area first.
  • 10 or below: Don't start sourcing yet. Go back to the client with what you need, or pass politely.

The numbers aren't magic. Their job is to make you ask the questions every time instead of only when you remember.

How to say "not yet" without losing the client

Passing on an order doesn't have to cost you the relationship. Be specific about what would change your answer:

"I want to make sure I can deliver on this. Right now the pay range is below what I'm seeing for this skill set, and without a call with the hiring manager it's hard to sell the role to candidates. If we can adjust either one, I'll make it a priority."

That kind of message shows you understand the market and respect their time. Many clients come back with a better order, and some come back with the next role first.

Use AI to prep, not to decide

AI can help you get ready for an intake call. Paste in the job description and what you know about the company, and ask for the questions that would uncover urgency, decision process and realistic requirements. You can also ask it to compare the requirements with the pay range and flag anything that looks out of line. Then you verify it against what you actually see in your market.

The bottom line

Your time is the most limited resource on your desk. Qualify every order on urgency, access, realism and terms, score it, and put your best effort where it has the best chance of becoming a placement. Fewer, better job orders is how a desk turns reqs into revenue.
